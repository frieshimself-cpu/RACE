Attempt: A black-box lower bound showing a natural “assignment-sampling” strategy for SAT cannot work

Setup
- Consider the following black-box model for SAT. An instance is represented by an unknown Boolean function f:{0,1}^n -> {0,1}, accessible only via queries. A query x∈{0,1}^n returns f(x).
- The goal is to decide the promise problem:
  YES: there exists exactly one x with f(x)=1
  NO: for all x, f(x)=0
- This models a large class of “search-by-sampling-assignments” approaches to SAT that only use evaluations of the CNF on complete assignments and do not exploit internal structure of the formula.

Theorem
Any randomized algorithm that, given oracle access to f, solves the above promise problem with success probability at least 2/3 in the worst case must make q = Ω(2^n) oracle queries. More precisely, for success probability at least 2/3 one needs q ≥ 2^{n-1}/3.

Proof
- By Yao’s minimax principle, it suffices to show that any deterministic q-query algorithm has average success probability at most 1/2 + q/2^{n+1} against a fixed hard input distribution.
- Define distribution D over functions f as:
  with probability 1/2: f ≡ 0 (NO instance),
  with probability 1/2: f = 1_u, where u is uniform in {0,1}^n and 1_u(x)=1 iff x=u (YES instance).
- Fix any deterministic adaptive algorithm A that makes at most q queries. Let the (random) queried points be X_1, X_2, ..., X_q, where X_t can depend on prior answers.
- Condition on f drawn from D.
  • If f≡0, all answers are 0; A’s transcript is all zeros, and A outputs some fixed decision b_0 determined by that transcript. Its success probability on f≡0 is 1 if b_0 = NO and 0 otherwise.
  • If f=1_u, then the transcript is all zeros unless A queries u; the probability (over uniform u) that A hits u in q queries is exactly q/2^n (regardless of adaptivity, since before seeing a “1” all queries are fixed strings and u is uniform over 2^n possibilities).
    – With probability 1 - q/2^n the transcript is again all zeros, leading A to output the same b_0.
    – With probability q/2^n it observes a “1” and can output YES and be correct.
  Therefore, A’s success probability on the YES case (averaged over uniform u) is at most q/2^n + (1 - q/2^n)·1{b_0=NO}.
- Averaging the two cases (each with probability 1/2 under D), the overall average success probability of A against D is:
  (1/2)·1{b_0=NO} + (1/2)·[q/2^n + (1 - q/2^n)·1{b_0=NO}]
  = (1/2)·q/2^n + 1{b_0=NO}·(1 - (1/2)·q/2^n - 1/2).
- This is maximized by setting b_0=NO, yielding
  success ≤ 1/2 + q/2^{n+1}.
- By Yao, any randomized algorithm with q queries has worst-case success probability at most 1/2 + q/2^{n+1}. To achieve ≥ 2/3 success, one needs
  1/2 + q/2^{n+1} ≥ 2/3 ⇒ q ≥ 2^{n-1}/3.

Implication for P vs NP efforts
- This shows that any approach to SAT that only treats the instance as a black-box mapping from assignments to truth values (i.e., relies on querying complete assignments, even adaptively and randomly) cannot yield a polynomial-time algorithm; it requires exponentially many queries in the worst case.
- Consequently, proof strategies that attempt to separate P from NP by demonstrating limitations or efficiencies of such black-box, assignment-sampling methods alone cannot resolve P vs NP: they must crucially exploit internal structure of the formula (syntax, small-width structure, algebraic or spectral properties, etc.), beyond mere assignment evaluations.

Limitations and scope
- The lower bound is tight up to constants and is unconditional.
- It applies to a promise version (unique-solution vs none), which is strictly easier than general SAT, strengthening the barrier.
- The result does not preclude non-black-box structural methods; it only rules out a natural, broad class of black-box search strategies. Thus it is a concrete analysis of why a plausible approach fails.
