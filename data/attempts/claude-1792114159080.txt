# Attempt: Hardness Magnification and the Locality Barrier for P vs NP

## Setup and goal

I focus on the *hardness magnification* program, currently one of the few
candidate routes to circuit lower bounds that is not obviously blocked by all
three classical barriers simultaneously. The phenomenon is that extremely weak
lower bounds for carefully chosen problems *amplify* to superpolynomial bounds
that would separate complexity classes. My contribution here is (1) a clean,
self-contained statement of a magnification threshold for a sparse variant of
MCSP, (2) a precise formulation of the **locality barrier** that explains why
the known weak lower bounds stop just short of the magnification threshold, and
(3) an observation that the barrier is not merely technical but is forced by a
compression property of the magnifying reduction itself.

## 1. A magnification statement

Let MCSP[s] be: given the truth table of a Boolean function on $n$ variables
(length $N = 2^n$), decide whether it has a circuit of size $\le s(n)$. Consider
the sparse / parameterized version with a gap, MCSP$[s, 2s]$ (promise: accept if
circuit size $\le s$, reject if $> 2s$).

**Known-type magnification theorem (schematic, as established by
Oliveira–Pich–Santhanam and McKay–Murray–Williams style arguments).**
*If for some $\varepsilon>0$ the problem MCSP$[2^{\sqrt n}]$ requires Boolean
circuits of size $N^{1+\varepsilon}$, then NP $\not\subseteq$ P/poly.*

So a lower bound *barely above linear* ($N^{1+\varepsilon}$) on the input length
$N$ yields a full separation. This is striking because we *do* have
near-quadratic lower bounds for some explicit functions in restricted models.
The question is why these do not already apply.

## 2. The locality barrier, made precise

The reductions that produce magnification share a structural feature. Write the
magnifying reduction as a family of circuits/formulas $R$ such that a small
circuit $C$ for the "easy" target is converted into a small circuit for the
"hard" target. The key property:

**Definition (ε-local reduction).** A reduction computing output bit $i$ of its
transform reads at most $t(N)$ bits of its input, each output bit's dependency
set computable in time $\mathrm{poly}(t)$, with $t(N) = N^{o(1)}$.

Essentially all current magnification reductions are $\varepsilon$-local: each
output coordinate depends on sub-polynomially many input coordinates (this is
what allows the gadget-based amplification to a *linear-size* object).

**Locality Barrier (formalization).** *No lower bound proof technique that only
certifies hardness via properties invariant under $\varepsilon$-local
reductions can cross the magnification threshold.* Concretely: every current
lower bound method (approximation method, polynomial method, communication
complexity of the right kind) defines a complexity measure $\mu$ that is
*robust under $\varepsilon$-local maps* — if $f = g \circ (\text{local map})$
then $\mu(f) \le \mathrm{poly}(t)\cdot\mu(g)$. The magnification target has
*small* $\mu$ by construction (it is built to be locally reducible from a trivial
object), so $\mu$ cannot witness its hardness.

This is why the gap is not accidental: the very locality that makes the
magnified bound small enough to be useful ($N^{1+\varepsilon}$) is the feature
that neutralizes the available measures.

## 3. A new observation: the barrier is a compression obstruction

I want to add something beyond restating the barrier. Consider *why* $\mu$ must
be local-robust for the known methods. Each such $\mu$ lower-bounds circuit size
by exhibiting a **certificate of hardness** $\Pi$ (e.g., an approximating
low-degree polynomial family, or a rectangle cover) whose size is itself
polynomially bounded in the circuit size. That is, the methods are
*constructive* in the Razborov–Rudich sense, and additionally **compressing**:
the certificate $\Pi$ has description length $\mathrm{poly}(\mu(f))$.

**Observation.** If a lower-bound method is compressing in this sense and the
target problem is produced by an $\varepsilon$-local magnifying reduction $R$,
then a hardness certificate for the target yields a hardness certificate for the
*source* of comparable size, by pulling $\Pi$ back through $R$ (local maps
compose with low-degree/rectangle structure without blowing up the certificate
beyond $\mathrm{poly}(t) = N^{o(1)}$). But the source is, by the design of
magnification, computationally trivial (it is where the "$2^{\sqrt n}$-size
circuit" input lives, of size subexponential and highly structured). Hence the
pulled-back certificate would certify hardness of a non-hard object — a
contradiction.

The upshot is a **clean necessary condition**: *any magnification-crossing proof
must use a hardness measure that is either non-constructive or
non-compressing under $\varepsilon$-local pullback.* This is strictly stronger
than "avoid natural proofs," because it rules out constructive-but-non-largeness
measures too, as long as they compress. It isolates exactly the property one must
sacrifice.

## 4. Why this is progress and where it stops

- It converts the vague sense that "magnification is close but blocked" into a
  concrete design requirement on the measure: *non-compressibility under local
  pullback*.
- It explains the empirical fact that approximation-method and polynomial-method
  bounds (near-quadratic in restricted models) never reach the magnification
  target: those measures are precisely compressing.
- It suggests a target: find a hardness measure that is provably
  non-compressing — e.g., one defined via *Kolmogorov-random* certificates with
  no short description, which would also be non-natural (no efficient
  largeness test), consistent with Razborov–Rudich.

**Where it fails to close the problem.** I have not exhibited such a measure, and
there is a genuine tension: a non-compressing measure typically is also hard to
*compute*, which usually means hard to *use* in a proof (one must still verify
the certificate certifies a lower bound). The observation reframes but does not
resolve the circularity: evading the locality barrier seems to require a measure
whose own evaluation is as hard as the problem it bounds. I cannot rule out that
this tension is fatal, and I have not proven the Locality Barrier formalization
in full generality (it is proven only for the enumerated concrete methods, by
the compression argument above; a fully general theorem would require a formal
model of "lower bound proof," which does not exist).

## Honest self-check

- The magnification statement is of the established form (I did not claim a new
  threshold).
- The compression observation (§3) is a correct consequence of two standard
  facts: (i) local maps preserve low-degree approximation / rectangle structure
  up to $\mathrm{poly}(t)$, and (ii) current methods produce certificates of
  size $\mathrm{poly}(\mu)$. Both hold for the approximation and polynomial
  methods; I did not verify it for *every* conceivable technique, so §2's
  "no technique" claim is only established for the enumerated ones.
- No separation is proven. This is a barrier-refinement / design-constraint
  result, not a resolution.
